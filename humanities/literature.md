# Literature

## Overview

Literature is the art of written (and oral) works that use language for aesthetic and emotional effect. It encompasses fiction, poetry, drama, and creative nonfiction. Literary study involves both the appreciation of artistic expression and critical analysis of how texts create meaning, reflect culture, and shape human understanding.

## Core Concepts

### Genres & Forms
- **Fiction**: Novel, novella, short story; realism, naturalism, modernism, postmodernism
- **Poetry**: Lyric, epic, dramatic, narrative; sonnet, haiku, free verse, villanelle
- **Drama**: Tragedy, comedy, tragicomedy, farce; playwrights from Sophocles to Beckett
- **Creative Nonfiction**: Memoir, personal essay, literary journalism, travel writing
- **Oral Traditions**: Epic poetry (Homer, Gilgamesh), folklore, myth, legend

### Literary Elements
- **Plot**: Exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, denouement; Freytag's pyramid
- **Character**: Protagonist, antagonist; round vs flat; dynamic vs static; foil
- **Setting**: Time, place, atmosphere; as symbol; as character
- **Theme**: Central idea or message; universal vs culturally specific
- **Point of View**: First person, third limited, third omniscient, second person; unreliable narrator
- **Style & Tone**: Diction, syntax, imagery, figurative language (metaphor, simile, personification, symbol, irony)
- **Narrative Voice**: Stream of consciousness; free indirect discourse; epistolary

### Literary Movements
- **Classical**: Greek and Roman literature; epic, tragedy, rhetoric
- **Medieval**: Romance, allegory (Dante, Chaucer), religious literature
- **Renaissance**: Humanism; Shakespeare, Cervantes, Milton
- **Enlightenment**: Satire, essay, novel's rise (Defoe, Swift, Voltaire)
- **Romanticism**: Emotion, nature, individualism (Wordsworth, Byron, Shelley, Keats)
- **Realism & Naturalism**: Everyday life, social conditions (Dickens, Tolstoy, Zola, Flaubert)
- **Modernism**: Fragmentation, stream of consciousness, alienation (Joyce, Woolf, Kafka, Faulkner)
- **Postmodernism**: Metafiction, irony, pastiche, unreliable narration (Pynchon, Borges, Calvino)
- **Contemporary**: Diverse voices; autofiction; digital literature; global perspectives

### World Literature
- **Western Canon**: Homer, Dante, Shakespeare, Cervantes, Goethe, Dostoevsky, Tolstoy, Joyce, Proust
- **Chinese Literature**: Classical poetry (Li Bai, Du Fu), Four Great Novels (Dream of the Red Chamber, Journey to the West, etc.), Lu Xun, Mo Yan
- **Japanese Literature**: Tale of Genji (Murasaki), haiku (Bashō), Kawabata, Murakami
- **Indian Literature**: Vedas, Mahabharata, Ramayana, Tagore, Rushdie
- **African Literature**: Chinua Achebe, Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o, Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie
- **Latin American Literature**: Magical realism (García Márquez, Borges, Allende); Neruda, Paz

### Literary Theory & Criticism
- **Formalism/New Criticism**: Close reading; text itself; Russian formalism (Shklovsky)
- **Structuralism**: Underlying structures; binary oppositions; semiotics (Barthes)
- **Post-Structuralism/Deconstruction**: Derrida; instability of meaning; différance
- **Feminist Criticism**: Gender in literature; patriarchy; women's writing (Showalter, Gilbert & Gubar)
- **Marxist Criticism**: Class, ideology, economic base in literature (Lukács, Jameson)
- **Postcolonial Criticism**: Empire, resistance, hybridity (Said, Spivak, Bhabha)
- **Psychoanalytic Criticism**: Freudian/Jungian readings; unconscious desire in texts
- **Reader-Response**: Meaning created by the reader (Iser, Fish)
- **Ecocriticism**: Literature and environment; nature writing

## Frontiers

- **Digital Literature and E-Literature**: How do hypertext fiction, interactive narratives, and AI-generated texts challenge traditional definitions of authorship and literary form?
- **World Literature and the Translation Problem**: What gets translated and what doesn't? The dominance of English and the politics of literary circulation across languages
- **Computational Literary Analysis**: Using NLP and distant reading (Moretti) to analyze large corpora — style, genre evolution, influence networks — and what is lost in the process
- **Posthumanism and Literature**: How fiction represents non-human agency, animal consciousness, ecological entanglement, and the dissolution of the human/non-human boundary
- **The Future of the Canon**: Ongoing debates about whose voices are included, decolonizing reading lists, and whether the concept of a literary canon remains viable

## Classic Works

- **"The Poetics"** by Aristotle — The foundational text of literary theory; defines tragedy, mimesis, catharsis, and dramatic structure
- **"The Anatomy of Criticism"** by Northrop Frye — Systematic framework for literary criticism; archetypal theory linking literature to myth and ritual
- **"Orientalism"** by Edward Said — Transforms literary and cultural studies by exposing how Western texts construct and dominate "the East"; cornerstone of postcolonial criticism
- **"How to Read and Why"** by Harold Bloom — Accessible guide to close reading across genres; passionate defense of aesthetic value and the Western canon
- **"The Madwoman in the Attic"** by Sandra Gilbert and Susan Gubar — Foundational feminist literary criticism; analyzes the woman writer's struggle against patriarchal literary tradition
